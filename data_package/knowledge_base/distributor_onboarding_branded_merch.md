# Distributor Onboarding — Branded Merchandise

For distributors new to carrying co-branded merchandise alongside their core beverage portfolio.

## Why this category is different

Branded merchandise is not a beverage. The selling cycle, the planning horizon, and the supplier relationship all differ from beer and spirits. Several distributors have learned this the hard way over the past two cycles, with consequences ranging from significant overstock write-offs to stockouts during peak promotional windows.

Three things to internalise before you place your first buy:

**Seasonality is sharp.** Apparel and headwear move strongly in summer; drinkware dominates Q4. Accessories are roughly flat. Unlike your beverage portfolio, where seasonal swings are 1.2x–1.4x trough to peak, merchandise routinely shows 2.5x–3.5x swings. If you plan a flat buy, you will be short in peak and long in trough.

**Promotions don't behave like beverage promotions.** A supplier-funded promotion on a beverage SKU lifts sales by maybe 1.2x–1.4x for the duration of the promo. On merchandise, lifts of 2x or more are routine, especially for co-marketing windows. This sounds appealing until you realise that a 2x lift on an inflated buy is what causes the overstock problem at the end of the window.

**The parent beverage signal matters.** Co-branded merchandise demand is partially driven by parent-brand beverage demand. If Crestmark Bourbon is having a strong quarter, expect Crestmark drinkware to follow. The lag varies by category — drinkware tends to be near-simultaneous, apparel and headwear lag the beverage by a few weeks. This is not always intuitive and is best discovered by examining your own historical data.

## Onboarding checklist

In your first cycle:

- Start with at most 6–8 SKUs per cluster. Do not try to carry the full range.
- Commit to a 90% sell-through target, not a coverage target.
- Tag every PO with the parent brand so you can reconcile against beverage sales later.
- Engage with the Category Manager monthly during your first two quarters.

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake new distributors make is treating merchandise as a side-bet — minimal planning, casual buys, no demand model. By the third quarter, the write-off bill is significant. The second most common mistake is over-correcting after the first overstock and under-buying the next peak. Both mistakes are avoidable with a proper planning process.
